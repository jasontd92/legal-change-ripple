  - The agent produced an 11-point rubric for whether a task needs a

  - Applied to our case, it splits the job in three:
    - Monitoring and diffing sources can be a plain scheduled pipeline, not an
      agent.
    - Checking one change against many documents is the part that's actually
      agentic: one coordinating agent with a worker per document.
    - Drafting the response is a single writing step, then a separate checker,
      then a pause for human approval.
  - You can defend that split in the review. The agent also proposed a test to
    back it up: run a single model call against the full agent and compare
    quality per dollar. That turns "does this need to be long-running?" into a
    measured answer.

  The framework the brief mentions
  - "Vercel's Eve" is almost certainly eve, an open-source framework released in
    June 2026 and still in beta.
  - It maps closely onto what GC AI said they value:
    - scheduled jobs, and sessions that survive restarts and long waits
    - approval pauses that cost nothing while waiting
    - subagents and skills
    - switching models with one config string, which makes comparing models easy
    - built-in evals, including LLM-graded checks
  - The risk is that it's beta and tied to Vercel.
  - "Pi" is a deliberately minimal alternative. It has no scheduling, durability
    or evals built in, so you'd add those yourself.

  Evals
  - GC AI publishes its own benchmark, the In-House Legal Bench. It has 100
    tasks, each graded on about 12 attorney-written criteria, and one category
    is regulatory tracking. Grading your eval set the same way would be a strong
    signal.
  - This is an open area. The agent found no public benchmark for how a
    regulatory change ripples through a company's contracts.
  - Planted changes have a precedent. A 2026 benchmark, CLAUSE, plants changes
    into real contracts and scores detection; the best model reached only about
    64%.
  - A starting eval design is also in its file: about 40–60 scenarios, at least
    25% of which should correctly produce no flag, scored on about 12

⏺ The vocabulary

  A contract between two businesses is usually a stack of documents, not one.

  Term: MSA — Master Services Agreement (or Master Supply / Purchase Agreement)
  What it is: The umbrella contract with a vendor or customer, signed once. It
  sets the permanent rules: price mechanics, liability caps (the most either
  side can owe), indemnities (who pays if a third party sues), confidentiality,
   termination, notice procedures, governing law.
  Engineer's analogy: A base class
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Term: SOW — Statement of Work (also order form, purchase order)
  What it is: A specific deal under the MSA: this project, this price, these
  dates. There can be dozens per MSA. It inherits the MSA's terms unless it
  overrides them.
  Engineer's analogy: A subclass or instance
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Term: Amendment
  What it is: A later signed change to the MSA or a SOW.
  Engineer's analogy: A patch
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Term: Order-of-precedence clause
  What it is: Says which document wins when they conflict, e.g. "the MSA
  controls
  unless a SOW expressly says otherwise."
  Engineer's analogy: Method resolution order
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Term: Incorporated by reference
  What it is: "Customer agrees to Vendor's online terms at [URL], as updated
  from
  time to time." A web page becomes part of the contract.
  Engineer's analogy: An unpinned dependency
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Term: Change-in-law / price-adjustment clause
  What it is: Says what happens to price or obligations if the law changes, e.g.

  "supplier may pass through new tariffs with 30 days' notice."
  Engineer's analogy: A config hook
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Term: Force majeure
  What it is: Excuses performance when extraordinary events occur (war,
  disaster). Courts rarely treat a tariff as one.
  Engineer's analogy: An exception handler
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Term: Notice clause
  What it is: How and when you must formally tell the other side something.
  Missing the window can forfeit the right.
  Engineer's analogy: A deadline on a callback
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Term: Policies / handbook
  What it is: The company's own internal rules. They must stay consistent with
  the law.
  Engineer's analogy: Internal config

  The main thing to know: a company has hundreds of these stacks. Each one
  divides up risk and creates obligations, and they reference each other.

  What a GC has to do when something changes

  Take an example. A 400-person plumbing company operates in CA, WA, IL and TX.
  A new tariff hits copper pipe, or a court strikes one down. Either way, the
  change comes from outside and the company's contracts don't change on their
  own.

  #: 1
  Legal step: Understand the change
  What the GC is actually doing: What changed? Is it final, stayed, or struck
  down? When does it take effect? Who does it apply to?
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  #: 2
  Legal step: Applicability
  What the GC is actually doing: Does it apply to us? This depends on states,
  industry, size thresholds and what we buy or sell.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  #: 3
  Legal step: Scoping
  What the GC is actually doing: Which of our ~300 documents could be touched?
  Here, supplier MSAs, purchase orders, fixed-price customer contracts,
  subcontracts.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  #: 4
  Legal step: Document-by-document analysis
  What the GC is actually doing: For each stack, read the relevant clauses. Does

  this supplier's MSA let them pass the tariff to us, and on how much notice?
  Does a customer SOW override the MSA's pricing? Does a defined term like
  "Taxes" include tariffs?
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  #: 5
  Legal step: Materiality & prioritization
  What the GC is actually doing: Which ones matter: dollars at stake, deadlines
  (a 30-day notice window), which contracts are easiest to act on.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  #: 6
  Legal step: Decide the response
  What the GC is actually doing: Per contract: do nothing, renegotiate, send a

  Technical walkthrough, mapped to the legal steps

  INPUTS: company profile · custom prompt · contract/policy corpus · change 
  source (URL or old/new pair)

  [A] Change intake ───────────── pipeline, not agent ─────────► legal step 1
  [B] Applicability & scope ──── one LLM call + retrieval ─────► steps 2–3   ◆ 
  optional human check
  [C] Corpus graph (built once) ─ parse + link documents ──────► the hierarchy 
  steps 3–4 depend on
  [D] Fan-out analysis ────────── orchestrator + N workers ────► step 4  (the 
  long-running core)
  [E] Verifier ────────────────── separate model pass ─────────► the "never 
  trust an unverified cite" rule
  [F] Synthesis ───────────────── single thread ───────────────► step 5
  [G] Approval gate ───────────── job pauses, costs nothing ───► step 6  ◆ GC 
  decides
  [H] Drafting ────────────────── approved items only ─────────► step 7 (drafts,
  never sends)
  [I] Tracking (ext.) ─────────── durable timers, re-runs ─────► step 8

  [A] Change intake.
  - Fetch the URL, snapshot it, and diff it against the last snapshot.
  - One LLM call turns the diff into a structured change record: what changed,
    effective date, legal status (proposed / final / stayed / struck down), and
    who it applies to.
  - The record is appended to the change log, deduplicated if it's been seen
    before.

  [B] Applicability & scoping.
  - The change record, company profile and custom prompt produce two things:
    - an "applies to us?" verdict with reasoning
    - a search plan: which document types and clause types could be affected
  - A quick retrieval over the corpus index then returns the candidate list of
    document stacks.

  [C] Corpus graph.
  - Parse every document and split it into clauses.
  - Extract defined terms.
  - Link the stacks: MSA → SOWs → amendments → incorporated URLs, plus the
    precedence clause.
  - This is what lets a worker answer "the SOW is silent, so the MSA's price
    clause governs."

  [D] Fan-out.
  - An orchestrator spawns one worker per document stack.
  - Worker tools: read document, search clauses, resolve defined term, follow
    cross-reference, check precedence.
  - Each worker writes a structured finding:
    - affected? (yes / no / needs review)
    - verbatim clause quote + location
    - the effect in plain English
    - severity
    - deadline
    - recommended action
  - Findings are persisted to disk as they're produced, so a crash resumes
    instead of restarting.
  - Workers are independent, so parallel is safe.

  [E] Verifier.
  - A different model checks that every quote exists word for word and actually
    supports the claim.
  - Failures are downgraded to "needs review" or dropped.
  - A fabricated citation is the one failure a lawyer won't forgive.

  [F] Synthesis.
  - One thread, so the ranking is consistent, turns all findings into an impact 
    register, ranked by severity and deadline, with:
    - a one-paragraph summary
    - a coverage statement: reviewed 287, couldn't parse 3, out of scope 10

  [G] Approval gate.
  - The job stops. The GC accepts or rejects each item and picks the response.
  - Durable execution means waiting days costs nothing.

  [H] Drafting. Notices and redlines, only for approved items, in the GC's
  template or voice. They're output as files; nothing is emailed.

  [I] Tracking (extension).
  - Each item gets an owner, status and due date.
  - Timers re-alert before deadlines.
  - A new change-log entry or a newly uploaded contract re-runs only the
    affected parts.

  Evals attach to each stage. Each stage has its own measurable output, so the
  evals naturally have several dimensions rather than one pass/fail:

  ┌─────────┬────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  Stage  │                             Metric                             │
  ├─────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ A       │ Change-record accuracy, including status                       │
  ├─────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ B       │ Applicability correctness; scope recall                        │
  ├─────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ D       │ Affected-clause recall and precision; false-alarm rate on      │
  │         │ "should not flag" cases                                        │
  ├─────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ E       │ Citation faithfulness (hard gate)                              │
  ├─────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ F       │ Severity and ranking calibration                               │
  ├─────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ H       │ Draft quality (rubric judge)                                   │
  ├─────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Overall │ Cost, latency, consistency across repeated runs                │
  └─────────┴────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  What makes a good trigger for this build

  What CUAD is (the "Kuad" you mentioned)

  not synthetic.
  - Contents: 510 commercial contracts, each as a PDF and a TXT file.
  - Labels: more than 13,000 annotations across 41 clause types. Those include
    liability cap, most-favored-nation, price restrictions, termination, notice
    and renewal periods, governing law, change of control, audit rights and
    insurance.
  - Who made it: The Atticus Project's attorney advisors and law students
    labeled it, in 2021. The license is CC BY 4.0, and it's on Hugging Face as
    theatticusproject/cuad.
  - Contract types: about 25, including supply, distribution, manufacturing,
    service, license and franchise.
  - Where the contracts come from: I believe they're public SEC filings, but the
    pages I checked didn't say so. I've marked that [VERIFY] in the doc.

  It has two limits for your use:
  - No contract families. Each contract stands alone, so you won't find an MSA
    together with its purchase orders and amendments.
  - Redacted pricing. Pricing is often blacked out as [***], which matters
    because your materiality depends on dollar amounts.

  Other sources for a realistic vault

  - SEC EDGAR full-text search. It's free and covers 2001 to today. Public
    companies file their important contracts as "Exhibit 10," and they keep
    filing amendments to the same agreement over the years. For one company you
    can rebuild a real family: the agreement, then Amendment 1, 2, 3. That's the
    closest free thing to a vault where the documents actually relate to each
    other. Copper-tube and plumbing manufacturers or distributors (Mueller
    Industries, Ferguson, Watsco) are candidates, though I haven't checked which
    of them filed useful contracts. Pricing is redacted here too.
  - Public-sector contracts. Cities and counties publish their contracts as
    public records. That includes "on-call plumbing services" master agreements
    with their task orders, amendments and change orders, which is exactly the
    MSA-and-child-documents structure. They're often scanned PDFs, so the
    messiness is real.
  - Supplier terms of sale published on the web. Distributors post their
    standard terms as HTML pages. These are real documents that purchase orders

  - Synthetic documents, used narrowly. Short child documents (purchase orders,

⏺ Short answer: yes, there are real second-order effects, and they are
  established legal concepts. But once you're precise about what "ripple" means,
  the build turns out to need three levels of document relationship. Only the
  third is a true cross-document graph, and you can defer it without making the
  corpus less realistic.

  First, a clarification that changes the question

  In your scope (steps 1–5), no document ever gets updated. Amending contracts
  is step 7. So the literal chain "case law changes, doc A gets amended, doc B
  is now affected" can't happen inside this build.

  What can happen is a dependency between findings. What you conclude about doc
  A becomes a fact you need in order to analyse doc B. For example: "Supplier X
  can pass the tariff through on 30 days' notice" is the finding on A. That
  finding is what makes a fixed-price customer contract B materially exposed.
  B's text doesn't mention tariffs. It becomes material only because of A.

  The three levels

  Level: L0: one document at a time
  What it is: Read a PO alone
  Graph?: No
  Needed?: Not viable. A PO can't be read without its MSA. Legally, it isn't the

  whole contract.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Level: L1: one contract stack at a time
  What it is: The MSA, its POs and SOWs, its amendments, and any terms
  incorporated by reference, read together
  Graph?: A small tree per counterparty, known and fixed
  Needed?: Required. The Incoterms, defined-term and precedence hops all live
  here.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Level: L2: effects across stacks
  What it is: A finding in one stack becomes an input to another stack with a
  different counterparty
  Graph?: A real graph, dependent on the findings
  Needed?: This is the "rippling" Jonathon described

  L1 is still "single change → per-document analysis" in spirit. The unit of
  analysis is just the contract stack rather than the file. L1 does involve a
  graph, but it's the static parent–child structure: you assemble context by
  following it. You never have to find new effects along it.

  Where L2 effects show up (tariff domain)

  These are established legal mechanisms. How often each one matters is my
  judgment, not sourced data.

  Mechanism: Pass-through squeeze
  Stack A (connected to the trigger): Supplier MSA allows the tariff to be
  passed
  through to us
  Stack B (doesn't mention the trigger): Fixed-price customer contract: we
  absorb
  the cost
  Would scoping find B on its own?: Partly. A good scope step flags customer
  contracts, but B's materiality depends on A's finding.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Mechanism: Most-favoured-nation (MFN) cascade
  Stack A (connected to the trigger): We raise the price to Customer X under its

  change-in-law clause
  Stack B (doesn't mention the trigger): Customer Y's MFN clause says Y gets the

  best price anyone gets. Raising X's price might be fine, but a concession to
  X triggers Y.
  Would scoping find B on its own?: No. Y's contract has nothing to do with
  tariffs.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Mechanism: Flow-down / back-to-back
  Stack A (connected to the trigger): Our prime contract with a customer changes

  our obligations
  Stack B (doesn't mention the trigger): A subcontract written to mirror that
  prime contract
  Would scoping find B on its own?: No. It's linked through the prime contract,
  not the trigger.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Mechanism: Refund chain (Trigger B)
  Stack A (connected to the trigger): The tariff was struck down, so refunds
  flow
  to the importer of record
  Stack B (doesn't mention the trigger): Our supplier passed the tariff through
  to us, so we may be owed part of their refund. We passed surcharges to
  customers, so they may be owed part of ours.
  Would scoping find B on its own?: Partly. And it runs in the opposite 
  direction
  to the pass-through.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Mechanism: Exclusivity blocks the fix
  Stack A (connected to the trigger): We want to switch to a domestic supplier
  to
  avoid the tariff
  Stack B (doesn't mention the trigger): The current supplier's MSA has
  exclusivity or minimum-purchase terms
  Would scoping find B on its own?: No. It only matters once someone considers
  switching suppliers, which is step 6.
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Mechanism: Financing covenants
  Stack A (connected to the trigger): Absorbed tariff costs squeeze margins
  Stack B (doesn't mention the trigger): The credit agreement's financial
  covenants and material-event notice duties
  Would scoping find B on its own?: No. It also needs financial data, not
  contract text.

  Your "bidirectional" intuition is partly right, but the dependencies run in a
  direction. They follow the supply chain from upstream supplier to us to
  downstream customer, and on Trigger B the direction reverses as refunds flow
  back up the chain. That is a graph, and building it would be real engineering.

  What this means for your two decisions

  1. Corpus: use a single entity, independent of the scope decision. A single
  company is more realistic, and I think it's also required, not just
  preferable. Step 3 (scoping) is one of the core capabilities you're
  evaluating. If each eval's corpus holds only the relevant documents, scope
  recall is meaningless, because nothing irrelevant exists to filter out. A
  single company gives you:
  - Relevant stacks: 3–5 supplier stacks, customer contracts (commercial
    fixed-price and consumer memberships), subcontracts, a credit agreement, and
    the distributor terms pages.
  - Realistic distractors: leases, NDAs, software licences, employment
    documents, and force-majeure clauses that look relevant but aren't.

  Each eval scenario is then a trigger applied to that one company, with planted
  variations applied as controlled edits to copies. So you get realism and
  per-eval control together.

  2. Scope: build L1 as the core, and put L2 cases in the corpus now.
  - L1 as the core. It's defensible, it already needs real agentic work
    (following stack hierarchies and deciding which hop to take next), and it
    gives you clean ground truth for each stack.
  - L2 in the corpus from day one. Plant a few L2 cases (an MFN pair, one
    flow-down, one pass-through squeeze) even though the core won't catch them.
    Your eval then measures what the core misses, which gives you:
    - a limitation you can state honestly in the write-up ("catches X% of
      first-order effects; blind to MFN cascades by design")
    - a ready-made test set if you build the extension
    - a strong talking point for the review, because it shows you understood the
      problem beyond what you built
  - An optional middle step (still a scope choice, not an architecture choice): 
    one-hop L2 through named link types only. After the per-stack findings are
    done, check only explicit link clauses (MFN, flow-down, back-to-back)
    against those findings. That's bounded and testable, and it demos the
    rippling effect Jonathon described without committing you to general graph
    traversal.

  A caution on L2 beyond one hop: it drifts into step 6. The refund-chain and
  covenant cases need economic reasoning and financial data, not just contract
  text. That's a sign they belong with the judgment extension, not the core.

  One more framing point. Jonathon's literal phrase was "understand one change's
  effect on other tracked documents." Strictly, that's L2. L1-core with L2
  measured matches his framing better than you might expect: you can show
  exactly where the ripple goes and which parts you catch.

  Once you decide between L1 only, L1 with one-hop L2, and full L2, I'll add the
  level taxonomy and your choice to take-home-context.md.

⏺ Yes, it changes things. The supplier chain is only one way a change spreads
  between contracts, and tariffs happen to lean on it more heavily than most
  legal changes. Here's the general picture.

  The within-contract level generalizes fully. I'll call it L1: reading one
  contract stack (an MSA, its purchase orders and SOWs, amendments, and any
  terms it pulls in by reference) as a single unit. Every contract in every
  legal domain is structured this way, so L1 depends on the kind of change not
  at all.

  The cross-contract level (L2) has many link types, and which ones matter 
  depends on the change. L2 is when a conclusion about one contract changes the
  analysis of another. Supply-chain cost flow is only one link type:

  ┌─────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │      Link type      │         Example that isn't about tariffs          │
  ├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Risk allocation     │ A ruling that makes freight brokers liable for    │
  │ (who pays if        │ how they pick carriers. It flows into the         │
  │ someone gets sued)  │ shipper's indemnity, the carrier's                │
  │                     │ counter-indemnity, and insurance.                 │
  ├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Copying down to     │ Clauses a government or prime contract requires   │
  │ subcontracts        │ to be copied into subcontracts                    │
  ├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ "No less favorable  │ Any most-favored-customer term                    │
  │ than" (MFN) clauses │                                                   │
  ├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │                     │ A ruling that voids class-action waivers hits     │
  │ Same template       │ every contract signed from that template. The     │
  │                     │ shared origin is the link, not the counterparty.  │
  ├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │                     │ A noncompete ban makes NDAs, non-solicits and     │
  │ Backup protection   │ IP-assignment agreements the main protection. Are │
  │                     │  they strong enough?                              │
  ├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │                     │ Privacy policy ↔ DPAs ↔ what customer contracts   │
  │ Consistency         │ promise. Employee handbook ↔ offer letters ↔      │
  │                     │ arbitration agreements.                           │
  ├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Same party          │ A counterparty or its affiliates is sanctioned or │
  │                     │  goes bankrupt                                    │
  ├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Timing chains       │ A notice received under one contract starts a     │
  │                     │ deadline under another                            │
  └─────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  What this means in practice:
  - Most case-law changes are about one kind of clause: whether it can be
    enforced, or how courts read it. For those, the main effect on each contract
    depends on three things:
    - whether the clause is present
    - which jurisdiction's law governs the contract
    - the facts

    That's L1 work run across the whole portfolio. When these changes do spill
    over to other contracts, it's mostly through backup protection, consistency,
    same-template or risk-allocation links, not the supply chain. This is my
    own reading, not a sourced finding.
  - The links are specific to each change. A noncompete ruling activates
    different links than a tariff does. So nothing in the problem statement
    should assume a fixed graph of supplier relationships. I've written that
    into the doc as a constraint on the problem. How to handle it is a question
    for the design session.
  - Jurisdiction and governing law are properties of a contract, not links 
    between contracts. They decide scope and whether the change applies at all,
    but they don't spread effects from one contract to another.

  What this means for the eval:
  - Tariffs over-represent the supply-chain pattern. If they're your only
    trigger, you'd be testing the least typical way a case-law change spreads.
    The brief also judges how well the approach generalizes.
  - A cheap fix is a small second set of eval cases built on a
    clause-enforceability trigger, run against the same company. For a plumbing
    company, a noncompete ban that covers hourly technicians is a good fit. The
    exception for noncompetes signed when someone sells a business works as a
    decoy.
  - The single-company corpus pays off twice. The NDAs and employment agreements
    you add as noise for the tariff trigger become the relevant documents for
    the noncompete trigger. You'd get a generalization test without building a
    second corpus.
